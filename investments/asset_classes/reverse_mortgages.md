# Reverse Mortgages

## Purpose

Reverse mortgages are meant to be a way for older Americans to utilize their home equity without having to move out of their house.  


## Eligibility

According to [the FTC website on reverse mortgages](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/reverse-mortgages), typical reverse mortgages require:
- borrower must be 62 or older
- live in the property used as collateral, which must be your primary residence
- have significant equity (own most of your home)
- have income to maintain the property during the reverse mortgage period (insurance/taxes)
- participate in a counseling session with [a HUD counselor](https://answers.hud.gov/housingcounseling/s) to determine suitability
- not have outstanding federal debt, like unpaid taxes (or other risks for asset garnishment or forfeiture)

## Benefits

### Access home equity without requiring loan payments

A typical home equity loan will require payments, which likely requires a sufficiently high income to qualify.

### Non-taxed income

Loans are not taxed as income, so the reverse mortgage payments to the borrower won't increase taxes on their Social Security benefits or increase their income bracket for Medicare costs.  The reverse mortgage payments may, however, prevent the borrower from qualifying for Medicaid of SSI due to valuing the reverse mortgage payments as liquid assets.


## Risks

### High cost of capital

Reverse mortgages typically have closing costs and mortgage insurance (covering the difference of the home sale value and the amount owed to the lender) in addition to the compounding interest.  These can significantly add to the costs of accessing your home's equity when compared to alternatives, like a home equity line of credit.

### Debt and interest accrual

Reverse mortgages are a form of debt; further, the amount owes increases over time, rather than decreasing with payments as in a typical (i.e., "forward") mortgage.  You can lose all of your home's value without being paid out all of the equity in your home.

### Limited housing options afterward

Once you take out a reverse mortgage, you must pay the balance (or, up to the amount earned on the home sale) if you move to a new home.  This means that you may lose the ability to downsize without a new mortgage later on, as the equity in your home has already been used up in the reverse mortgage.  Alternatively, you will likely lose the ability to pass on the home to heirs, as the full balance (or, again, the amount received from a sale) must be paid when the borrower passes, leaving little time for heirs to come up with the cash needed to keep the home.

### Maintenance, taxes, and insurance liability

Borrowers must still maintain their home, stay up-to-date on property taxes, and keep an active home insurance policy, or else they risk losing their home.  If a borrower is illiquid enough to consider a reverse mortgage, it must seriously be considered if they have sufficient income and ability to care for the property over the term of the reverse mortgage.
